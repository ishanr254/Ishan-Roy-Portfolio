Transitioning the team to a carbon fiber fuselage was a two year process that I started as Mechanical and Structural Subteam Lead. In prior years, the fuselage had been constructed out of a semi-monocoque wood and MonoKote structure. However, this method led to heavy designs that were prone to structural failures. In prior years, structural failures had been a significant contributor to unsuccessful competition outcomes along with the heavy aircraft being difficult to control.

. 

Having led the mechanical and structural subteam the previous year, where the team's first composite fuselage was designed and fabricated reducing aircraft weight by 45%, I directed the team to carry forward and mature that approach for DF1.

The final configuration employs a CFRP monocoque consisting of two 3-ply shells fastened together with nylon hardware. Each shell uses alternating 45-degree ply orientations to improve torsional stiffness and load distribution. Three basswood bulkheads subdivide the fuselage into electrical, cargo, and passenger compartments, with basswood plates used at the wing and tail interfaces to distribute concentrated loads into the CFRP shell. Structural performance was verified using ANSYS ACP, evaluating a governing combined loading case that included a distributed wing attachment load of 200 lbf representing a worst-case scenario, longitudinal electronics and tail-induced moments, and a landing load from the landing gear. All other loads were calculated based on a structural limit load factor of 3.5. The composite failure tool indicated a specific FoS of 5.6 against this worst-case loading scenario.

The decision to carry significant structural margin was deliberate. A FoS of 5.6 is conservative for this class of aircraft, but given that the team's primary goal for the season was to field a reliable competition aircraft, the added confidence in structural integrity was judged to be worth the modest weight penalty.

### Layup Process & Manufacturing Improvements

Each fuselage shell is manufactured as a wet carbon fiber layup in a 3D-printed mold: impregnated CFRP sheets are layered with a peel-ply release film, a perforated release film, and fabric breathers, then cured in a vacuum bag for 18 hours. After demolding, shells are sanded and trimmed with a Dremel rotary tool to remove excess material and smooth the edges before the two halves are fastened together.

For 2025–2026, the vacuum-bagging process itself was refined: raising vacuum pressure to 30 inHg reduced excess resin and enabled sharper mold geometry than prior layups could achieve, cutting fuselage weight by **20%** while maintaining strength — a direct continuation of the composite fabrication work I started the prior season.
