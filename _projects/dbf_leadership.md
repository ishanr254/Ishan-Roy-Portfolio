### Role & Scope

As Full Team Co-Lead for the 2025–2026 AIAA Design Build Fly season, I directed **40 members across four subteams** with a **$35,000 operating budget**, responsible for all engineering decision-making and day-to-day operations. After two years on the team serving as a member and then mechanical subteam lead, I inherited the full team lead position after a rocky year where interpersonal issues amongst the full team leads caused detrimental effects throughout the year, ending in an unsuccessful competition. 

My co-lead and I rebuilt the team from the ground up, and were able to successfully implement new technical and team functionality directives. Under our leadership, the team successfully flew our aircraft at competition for the first time in 5 years, placed in the top 20% of all entrants for the first time in a decade, and began what we hope is a new era for CU DBF.

### 2025–2026 Competition Recap

This year's missions centered around creating a banner towing bush plane. Mission 1 required completing three laps in a payload-free configuration. Mission 2 scored teams on net income generated by carrying passengers (rubber ducks) and cargo (hockey pucks) across as many laps as possible in a five-minute window. Mission 3 involved deploying and towing a banner, with performance based on the number of laps flown and banner length. A fourth ground mission timed the team's ability to load passengers and cargo, then swap configurations to install the banner.

CU DBF successfully completed Mission 1 and 2 along with the ground mission. However, the team did not get to attempt Mission 3 due to weather delays caused by a Tornado warning. The team placed **33rd** out of 200 schools and even outperformed the top 3 teams in Mission 2.

### Lessons in Leadership

Managing the technical program was one thing; leading the people behind it was another. Here are two moments from the season that shaped how I think about engineering leadership.

### Refocusing the Team after the Preliminary Design Review (PDR)

The first design review in September, PDR, exposed how much work still needed to be done to improve team culture and raise the level after multiple years of low quality design work. My tenure as lead had just begun, but we'd spent the previous summer and the first month of school trying to drive home the changes the team needed to make to find success. However, my co-lead and I were distracted racing to finish the MDO before PDR, and we lost sight of what the rest of the team was doing in preparation for the review. During the review, we saw that most of our team's problems had not gone away. Designs were still overly complicated without much thought put toward implementation.

After the review, we sat down with our leadership team and laid out what we needed to change. After a month of work, we realized that everyone was new to leadership, and that we had not done a good job of being explicit in how we wanted the team run. Everyone was on board with trying to do better. They just did not know how. I was able to communicate all that I'd learned about leadership in my first couple months in the position in several sessions with the subteam leads. We made several changes including requiring agendas for work sessions, raising standards for CAD models and analysis, and encouraging subteam leads to focus on leading instead of managing. 

Over time, I began to see a shift in the mentality of the team. Every time we raised the standards for a design review or milestone, the team met them. This taught me a great deal about technical leadership, especially when motivating your team members is so important. I learned that people who are passionate about something will spend the time to do the extra work and improve themselves, but that you have to give them an avenue to do so. Before expectations were explicitly raised, people remained in their old habits. However, as soon as we gave them direction and a direct method for improving themselves, they met the challenge.

### A Flight Test Before Thanksgiving

The biggest technical leadership I had to make during the development of our aircraft was following the crash of the first iteration in the weeks before Thanksgiving break. The aircraft crashed due to a loss of signal because we had not realized that we needed to adapt our radio control system to work with our carbon fiber heavy plane. After the crash, there was one weekend left before everyone would leave for Thanksgiving break. From a timeline perspective, if we did not get a test flight in before Thanksgiving, it is likely that we would not get one in before January.

As we recovered the aircraft from the ditch it had dug itself, I began running through all that we'd need to do to rebuild the aircraft for another test the following weekend. It was going to be a heavy lift, requiring long hours, expedited part orders, and pushing the team really hard quite early in the year. It was 100% necessary though, and I knew that if the other leads were on board, we could inspire the team to work longer hours and push to get the plane ready in a week. We'd have to reallocate some funds to rush ship replacement components as well, but history reminded us that waiting too long for a first test flight would be devastating. We would lose valuable time to make the fixes to the design that hadn't even revealed themselves yet. 

That one week was one of the hardest of the year. Members stayed late laser cutting new parts and assembling an entire new wing and tail. I was proud of the fact that I was able to inspire the team to do so even early in the year when the deadline of competition seemed so far away. The next weekend, we flew successfully and landed our aircraft for the first time in multiple years. We proved our design worked and now had a test bed for the competition missions. More importantly, we could shift our focus to improving the design rather than trying to make it work in the first place. I personally believe this was a huge catalyst for the success that we had throughout the year.
