### Role & Team

I interned on GE Aerospace's Defense Engines and Services organization, on the Turbofan Turbojet (TFTJ) Mechanical Systems, Structures, and Configurations design engineering team in Lynn, MA, from May to August 2026, working across the F404 and F414 engine programs, with most of my work supporting the External Configurations and Structures groups.

Design engineering is the department that owns the engine hardware. Individual engineers are assigned specific parts and hold final engineering authority over them: they control what the drawings look like, substantiate whether a part that comes back outside drawing tolerance will still function correctly, and walk down suppliers to confirm parts are being manufactured the way they're intended. Safety comes first throughout, including making sure part manuals and inspection requirements reflect what the engineering team wants for that hardware. Every part on the engine has an engineer who owns it.

My team was about 15 people, reporting up through a manager to an executive, who in turn reported to the executive overseeing all of GE Aerospace's defense engineering.

### Leading a Part Fabrication Process Review

I led an effort to change the process classification of a part flagged in a prior product readiness review for a fabrication issue with potential field failure implications. I researched the part's fabrication and inspection history, then worked with subject matter experts and engine platform leads to determine which process designations actually applied to defense hardware, since the classification I initially pursued turned out not to. I revised the recommendation, built the case for the alternative path, and presented it to section executives, engine model engineers, and senior project engineers, ten people in the room and roughly forty more on the call. The proposal was approved both technically and programmatically, and I documented the full process in the team's design record book so it could be reused for similar parts going forward.

---

### Structural Substantiation and Nonconformance Reviews

Most of my internship was spent dispositioning nonconforming hardware: parts that come back from a supplier out of tolerance with the drawing, sometimes with a proposed repair, where engineering has to decide if it's acceptable to use as-is, repair, or scrap. I substantiated these dispositions with comparative stress analysis, calculating how much a nonconformance increased local stress relative to the factor of safety in the part's existing strength and life reports, tracing load paths to determine whether the affected region was load-bearing at all, and reviewing fit, function, repairability, and field maintainability. This spanned parts across several different engine programs and had me reading and interpreting a large number of engineering drawings, along with corresponding directly with suppliers to get the information needed to close out each review.

---

### Engineering Writing for a Compliance-Sensitive Audience

A lot of this work was ultimately reviewed by the Defense Contract Management Agency (DCMA), which meant learning a stricter standard of technical writing than I'd used before: state findings exactly as they are, never lead the reader toward a conclusion, include nothing beyond what's necessary, and never leave a reader guessing how a conclusion was reached. That standard, precise, complete, and neutral at once, is one I still apply to any technical write-up I do.

---

### Documentation and Drawing Standardization

I also worked with the systems engineering team to compare F404 inspection and maintenance manuals across different engine derivatives, checking that specifications in older manuals were still as safe and current as the newest ones. Separately, I traced a missing surface-finish callout on a legacy drawing back through historical specifications, determined the correct value, and pushed the fix through GE's drawing change process while coordinating directly with the manufacturing supplier on interim machining instructions.

---

### What This Taught Me

Presenting technical recommendations to section executives and platform leaders, several of these for the first time, taught me that a proposal's credibility is mostly set before you're in the room, by how thoroughly you've already worked through the questions someone senior is going to ask. Nonconformance work reframed how I think about engineering judgment more broadly: a repair or deviation is never just a stress problem, it's a load-path problem, a maintainability problem, and a communication problem at the same time, and treating it as only one of those makes the analysis incomplete.

### GE Aerospace & the F404/F414 Engine Family

GE Aerospace is a Fortune 500 aerospace company headquartered in Evendale, Ohio, with roughly 57,000 employees worldwide and about $46 billion in 2025 revenue. Its aviation business traces back to 1917, and it became an independent public company in 2024 after General Electric split into three companies: GE Aerospace, GE HealthCare, and GE Vernova. The company runs two segments, Commercial Engines & Services and Defense & Propulsion Technologies, and its installed base spans roughly 70,000 commercial and military engines flying worldwide.

The F404 has been in service since the 1980s, with more than 4,000 engines delivered, over 13 million flight hours logged, and F404-powered aircraft flying in 16 countries. It's flown combat on the Boeing F/A-18 Hornet and the F-117 Nighthawk, and was selected to power the U.S. Air Force's newest jet trainer, the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk, alongside the Saab JAS 39 Gripen, the KAI T-50/FA-50 family, and India's HAL Tejas Mark 1. The F414 shares the F404's core architecture, more than 5,600 F404 and F414 engines have been built between them, with a combined 18 million flight hours, but scales up to roughly 22,000 lb of thrust. It powers the Boeing F/A-18E/F Super Hornet and EA-18G Growler, the newer Gripen E/F, South Korea's KF-21 Boramae, India's upcoming Tejas Mark 2, and NASA's X-59 QueSST quiet supersonic research aircraft.
