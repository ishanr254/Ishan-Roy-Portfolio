### Project Overview

The Magnus Ultralight project was an investigation into the experimental aircraft concept of human-scale flight using the Magnus Effect, a spinning body that generates lift as it moves through airflow. The goal was to determine the feasibility of a person-sized eVTOL aircraft that uses a large spinning sphere to generate lift, capable of efficiently carrying a person over long distances.

I led a three-person team through this investigation, which ran from January 2025 through the end of the fall 2025 semester. The project began with a small-scale quadcopter, Prototype 1, used to validate and quantify the Magnus Effect as a lift generating method before committing to a full-scale design. Prototype 1 flew successfully and, over the summer of 2025, was able to lift the weight of the sphere actuation mechanisms, though it was never flight tested with the spheres attached. In parallel over that same summer, the project shifted to a hexacopter configuration, Prototype 2, to make room for the structural reinforcement the added weight required. Prototype 2 was designed across summer and fall 2025, then built and tested that fall with the spheres installed and spinning. Those tests showed that the Magnus Effect was not a viable way to generate the lift required to keep the aircraft in the air, which concluded the investigation. The development of both prototypes, and the result they produced, is documented below.

### Prototype 1

#### Overview and Results

Prototype 1 began as a standard quadcopter configuration for vertical takeoff, landing, and forward propulsion, designed by me before the summer of 2025. In its base configuration, without the added weight of the sphere actuation mechanisms, Prototype 1 flew properly, validating the core airframe and propulsion system.

Over the summer of 2025, my team and I iterated on that design to reduce weight, increase thrust, and integrate the Magnus Effect sphere actuation system. The spheres themselves went through several redesigns over the same period, converging on the final hub and mounting design described below. By the end of the summer, the updated prototype completed ten five-minute hovers while carrying 4 kg of payload, 25% above the original goal, validating that the vehicle could lift the weight of the sphere mechanisms. There wasn't time to flight test it with the actual spheres attached, so the sphere actuation system was ground tested instead, achieving a rotation rate of over 1,500 RPM.

The development of prototype 1 is documented below.

#### Iterative improvements
Throughout the fabrication and testing phase of the first prototype in summer 2025, several improvements were made to the design, many of which are carried over to prototype 2. They will be discussed here.

#### *Sphere Actuation Mechanism*
Further research and modeling into the drag characteristics of spinning bodies showed that the initial design for actuating the spheres would not be powerful enough to overcome the skin friction drag generated by the 24 in diameter spinning spheres. Given the high Reynolds number of the flow around a spinning sphere, the turbulent boundary layer model of skin friction drag was appropriate. Therefore, the torque necessary to spin each sphere scales by roughly the rotation rate raised to the fifth power. This necessitated more powerful motors than initially thought. However, finding motors that were both reasonably lightweight and powerful enough proved to be difficult, so gearboxes were used instead to gear down high speed lightweight motors.

Additionally, several methods for holding onto the spheres were tested over the course of the summer. This proved to be quite the challenge due to the tendency of the spinning sphere to bend away from its axis of rotation due to the centrifugal force generated. The final design involved a 3d printed hub with six spokes extending from it radially like a cone. The spokes were affixed to an inflated beach ball using epoxy, and provided enough stiffness to keep the ball spinning properly.

### Prototype 2

#### Overview

Alongside the summer 2025 testing, the project shifted to a hexacopter configuration. The quadcopter frame didn't leave enough room to add the structural reinforcement the added weight of the sphere mechanisms required, and a hexacopter's six arms provided the space needed to strengthen the frame without exceeding weight targets. The design was carried through the summer and into the fall.

#### Testing and Result

Prototype 2 was tested in fall 2025 with the spheres installed and driven by the gear-driven hub system. Across several test iterations using different aircraft configurations, the spinning spheres did not generate enough lift to sustain the aircraft in flight. The conclusion of the project was that the Magnus Effect is not a viable way to generate the lift required to keep an aircraft of this type in the air.

The result was negative, but the vehicle did the job it was built to do. Prototype 2 existed specifically to isolate and measure the lift contribution of the spinning spheres on a flying platform, and it produced a clear answer to the question the project set out to ask rather than leaving the concept untested. Reaching a definitive answer on an experimental concept with no prior airframe or precedent to build from was the outcome that mattered.

With that question answered, I transitioned leadership of the project back to Professor Peck at the end of the fall 2025 semester in order to focus on my role as Full Team Co-Lead of CU Design Build Fly.
